# The Router That Watches It Work

## Where Tierllama is, where it came from, and where it's going

### The idea

Every AI message today hits the biggest model available, whether or not it needs to. You pay frontier prices to draft a text, and the compute sits mostly wasted. **Tierllama flips that default.** A tiny, free, local classifier — we call it Jev — reads every incoming message in a few seconds, scores it on what kind of work it actually is, and routes it to the *best value* lane: your own hardware first, cheaper cloud models for medium work, expensive frontier models only when the job truly earns them. The thesis is not "cheapest" — it's "best value for that person's money at that moment." A task you need done *right now* routes to wherever answers fastest. A task for *later* queues onto your own machine and runs when your hardware is free. Routing, timing, and scheduling work together so that the compute you already own finally gets used for the work it can do — and the cloud bill only grows for the work that genuinely needs it. Measured on our own hardware: tiered routing saves 40–80% against always-best routing. Every accuracy point of the classifier is real money at scale — market math prices each point at roughly $30 per billion tokens. We're commoditizing intelligence, one correct routing decision at a time.

### What we've built so far

Tierllama is a working product core, not a slideware vision. The Jev brain (qwen3:8b, Apache-2.0, runs on a 6GB GPU) classifies **80.0% exact-tier accuracy** on a 211-message golden bench at a ~3s median — free, local, and improving weekly through a disciplined calibration process (rubric patches are one-liners, every change is bench-verified before it ships). The web dashboard turns the invisible into the visible: you can watch every routing decision, its confidence, its lane, its cost. A catalog oracle scrapes the provider's *full* live catalog — including models the user never knew existed — and the hardware gate honestly refuses to promise intelligence the machine can't currently deliver. The scheduler adds the time dimension: "do this by Friday" enters a real queue with a real deadline, and a clarify lane asks exactly one question when the classifier is genuinely unsure instead of silently guessing. Around it sits an escalation ladder (failed work moves up a tier), a capability loop that learns which model-classes fail on which prompt-classes, and a decision log that records every judgment with its evidence — because a router that can't show its reasoning is just a black box with an API key.

### Where we are right now

This is the dogfood season: **Tierllama is now classifying the real traffic of its own developing team.** The dogfood collector ships as a silent hook in our own chat gateway — every real message we send taps the router, and the tap never breaks the chat if the router is down. In its first live day it captured a real spread of work: medium now-rush tasks, hard deadline work, all graded by Jev with calibrated confidences. The next discipline is the one that matters: the developer agent compares its *own* read of each message against Jev's verdict, and those pairs — not vibes, not anecdotes — become the calibration fuel. Disagreement buckets feed the clarify lane, clarify lane answers feed the rubric, and the rubric only changes through the measured gate. This is also when we caught and fixed our first real-world perf law: the host's global context-length setting silently degraded a vision model to 6.6 tokens/sec and dragged the classifier down with it — the fix (explicit per-call context, measured at both bench time and run time) is the kind of lesson you can *only* learn by routing your own traffic and being hoisted by your own petard.

### Where we're heading

Three frontiers, in order of when they ship. First, **finish the learning loop**: dogfood agreement data accumulates, the clarify card (shipped today) makes answering ambiguity a one-tap act, and distilled *patterns* — never raw prompts — feed back into the rubric under human review. Privacy is a law, not a setting: full detail stays on the machine that produced it; only bucket-level insight ever crosses the boundary. Second, **the fleet**: your machines become one coherent worker pool. Machines get honest, user-confirmed classes; *later* work lands on the box that can chew it overnight; the canary probes test whether a model can actually do work a tier below its label and promote it when proven — savings found by evidence, not by price tag. And the wedge that makes this a business: today, every consumer LLM server on your network is invisible to every other one. Ollama punts security to reverse proxies; LM Studio and llama.cpp don't even try. **Tierllama will be the discoverable, authenticated, encrypted fleet node that none of them ship** — pair once, and your whole household's hardware becomes one routed brain. The niche is real, it's verified, and nobody owns it yet. The main goal, stated plainly: **make compute dramatically cheaper for everyone by re-routing the world's AI calls through the hardware people already own** — routed by what the work *is*, scheduled by when it's *needed*, and priced by what it *costs* — so that the cheapest useful answer and the best-value answer stop being different things.
